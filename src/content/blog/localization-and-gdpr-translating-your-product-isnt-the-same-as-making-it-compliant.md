## Translation isn't compliance — and that distinction matters

Let me be precise about the line between the two, because everything hangs on it. Compliance is a legal question. Does your data handling actually meet what the GDPR and French law require? That's for your legal team or your counsel to answer. No translator replaces them, and I'd be wary of anyone who says otherwise.

Localization is the layer on top. Once the legal requirements are settled, is the French wording clear, accurate, and right for a French user? That's where a translator who's used to legal text earns their place — because a technically correct translation of a consent request can still miss the thing the GDPR genuinely cares about: that consent be freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous.

So if your French consent copy is vague, buries the actual choice, or reads like it came out of a machine, it can undermine the validity of the consent itself. Doesn't matter that the underlying data practice was fine. The words are part of the compliance here, not a coat of paint on top of it.

## Where the two actually collide

A few places where localization and GDPR overlap, and where a rushed translation causes real trouble:
Consent language. The breezy "by continuing, you accept…" that passes in some markets doesn't clear the GDPR bar for active, specific, unambiguous consent. The French has to make the choice genuinely clear — not quietly usher people past it.

### **Privacy notices.**

These have to be readable by an ordinary person, in plain French. A dense, calque-ridden translation of your English policy fails the GDPR's own transparency standard, which asks for information that's concise, transparent, intelligible, and easy to get to. Four adjectives a word-for-word rendering rarely satisfies.

### **Cookie banners.**

French cookie rules, shaped by CNIL guidance, are strict and specific. "Accept" and "Reject" have to be presented fairly — and the French wording, right down to the framing, is doing legal work.
User-rights flows. Access, correction, deletion, portability. These are how people exercise actual legal rights, so the French can't be ambiguous. Confusing copy here isn't just clumsy UX; it can get in the way of a right someone is entitled to.

### **Tone, too.**

Even the tu/vous call touches this. Privacy and legal communication in French leans formal, and an over-casual register in a consent flow lands wrong exactly where you most need to feel trustworthy.

## What doing it well looks like

The teams that get this right stop treating legal and privacy copy as strings in the same pile as UI labels. They give it its own lane.

In practice, that means starting from the real legal requirements (ideally with your legal team defining what has to be said) and then localizing the wording of it clearly and faithfully. It means keeping privacy and consent terminology consistent across the whole product, the way you'd manage any other critical vocabulary in a glossary. It means reviewing this copy where it actually appears, not in a spreadsheet cell. And it means flagging early, before launch, anywhere the English quietly assumes something that doesn't hold under French or EU rules so it gets fixed in a planning meeting instead of after a complaint.

That's the judgment call a Language Lead is there to make: knowing which strings are ordinary interface text and which ones carry legal weight, and refusing to treat them the same.

## The takeaway

Translating your product gets it into French. Localizing your consent flows, your privacy notices, your legal copy (with the GDPR in the back of your mind the whole time) is what keeps that French version trustworthy.

They're not the same job. And in the one place where "close enough" comes with legal risk attached, the difference is very much worth getting right.
